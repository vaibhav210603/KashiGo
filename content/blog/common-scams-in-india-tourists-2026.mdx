# Common Scams in India for Tourists (2026): What to Watch for in Every City

I want to start with something important: India is not a country of scammers. The vast majority of people you meet — the chaiwala, the family on the train, the shopkeeper who gives you directions — are kind and genuinely curious about you. Many will help you without wanting a single rupee.

But the tourist trail is different. On the classic route — Delhi, Agra, Jaipur, Varanasi, Goa — there are people whose full-time job is to separate first-time visitors from their money. They are good at it, and they use the same handful of tricks everywhere.

I was born in the galis (lanes) of Kashi and I've spent my life watching foreigners arrive in Varanasi after two weeks on the road, already tired of being tricked. This post is the overview I wish they'd read before landing. For Varanasi specifically, I've written a separate, detailed guide on [how to avoid scams in Varanasi](/blog/how-to-avoid-scams-in-varanasi).

## The Pattern Behind Almost Every Scam

Before the city-by-city list, learn this. Nearly every scam in India has three parts:

1. **Unasked help** — someone approaches you and offers something you didn't request.
2. **An information gap** — they know something you don't: the real price, the real rule, whether the place is actually open.
3. **Time pressure** — you must decide now. The office is closing, the train is leaving, the offer ends today.

When you notice all three at once, stop. Step away, check for yourself, and decide slowly. That one habit protects you from most of the scams below.

## Arrival: Airports and Stations

### The taxi that takes you somewhere else

You land late, you're tired, and a driver outside arrivals says your hotel is "closed," "overbooked" or "burned down last week." He knows a better one. That better one pays him commission, and your room costs double.

**How to avoid it:** Use the official prepaid taxi counter inside the airport, or a ride-hailing app like Uber or Ola. Call your hotel yourself if anyone tells you it's closed. Pre-book a pickup with your hotel for a late-night arrival.

### The "broken meter"

Your taxi or auto driver says the meter is broken and quotes a flat price that's three times normal.

**How to avoid it:** Agree the price before you get in, or use an app so the fare is fixed. If a driver won't agree a price, there will be another one in 30 seconds.

### The fake SIM seller

Outside some airports and stations, people sell "tourist SIMs" that turn out to be overpriced, never activated, or registered to someone else.

**How to avoid it:** Buy a SIM at an official store of a major network (Airtel, Jio, Vi) or a proper counter inside the airport. You'll need your passport and visa, and activation can take a few hours.

## Delhi

### The fake "government tourist office"

This is Delhi's most famous scam. Near New Delhi railway station and Connaught Place, someone directs you to the "official tourist office." It looks official — maps, flags, a desk — but it's a private travel agency that sells hugely overpriced tours, cars and train tickets.

**How to avoid it:** Real government tourism offices do not send people to grab you on the street. Book trains on the official IRCTC site or app, or at the actual reservation office.

### "The station is closed" / "The ticket office is upstairs"

At New Delhi station, a man stops you before you enter and says the foreign tourist booking office has moved, the station is closed for a ceremony, or your ticket is "not confirmed." He sends you to a fake office outside.

**How to avoid it:** Walk past and go inside. The station is not closed. Nobody needs to stop you at the entrance to check your ticket.

### The ₹10 tuk-tuk tour

An auto driver offers to show you around the whole city for ₹10 or ₹50. The catch: the day is spent at "emporiums" and shops where he earns commission.

**How to avoid it:** If the price is impossibly low, you're the product. Say clearly "no shops" before you get in.

## Agra

### Fake Taj Mahal guides and ticket touts

Around the Taj Mahal, men in semi-official clothing say you must hire a guide, buy tickets from them, or that the gate you're at is closed. Some sell fake or overpriced tickets.

**How to avoid it:** Buy tickets only at the official ASI counters or the official online booking site. Guides are optional. Remember the Taj Mahal is closed to general visitors on Fridays.

### The "free" marble inlay workshop

After the Taj, someone offers to show you how the marble inlay work was done — just a free demonstration. It ends in a showroom with heavy pressure to buy "genuine" pieces at very high prices.

**How to avoid it:** Enjoy the demonstration if you want, but decide before you go in that you won't buy on the spot.

## Jaipur

### The gem export scam

This one has cost travellers thousands of dollars. A friendly young man befriends you over a few days, then explains that you can make money by carrying gems abroad to "avoid export tax" and selling them to his contact. You pay for the gems with your card. The gems are worthless and the contact never appears.

**How to avoid it:** There is no legitimate deal in which a stranger needs you to carry gems for profit. If anyone mentions export tax, gems and your credit card in the same conversation, leave.

## Goa and the Beaches

Goa is relaxed, but watch for overpriced taxi fares (agree the price first), rental scooter "damage" claims (photograph the scooter all over before you ride), and drinks bills that don't match what you ordered. Keep valuables off the beach when you swim.

## Trains

- **"Train cancelled":** Someone at the station says your train is cancelled and offers a car or bus instead. Check the departure board or the official app.
- **Fake porters and fake ticket checkers:** Real TTEs (ticket examiners) have badges and are on the train. Agree porter fees before they touch your bag.
- **Luggage theft at night:** Lock your bag to the berth with a cable lock in sleeper and 3AC classes.

## Money: ATMs, Change and Prices

- **ATM helpers:** Never accept help at an ATM. Cover your PIN. Use machines inside banks or malls.
- **Short change:** Count your change before you walk away, especially with ₹500 notes.
- **MRP:** Packaged goods have a printed MRP (Maximum Retail Price). Nobody can legally charge more for that item. Check it on water bottles and snacks.
- **UPI:** Most Indians pay by UPI (phone payment). Some services now let foreign visitors use UPI with a prepaid wallet, but carry cash as a backup, especially in old cities.

Varanasi has its own classics: the burning ghat "wood donation," fake guides practising their English, inflated boat prices, sadhus charging for photos, and silk shop commission detours. One useful fact: the only official India Tourism office in Varanasi is at 15B The Mall, in the Cantonment area. Anyone pointing you to a "government tourist office" in the old city is sending you to a private agent.

## What Is Usually NOT a Scam

Not every approach is a trick. Families, especially in smaller towns, will often ask for a photo with you. That's nearly always just curiosity and friendliness. People asking "Which country?" are usually just making conversation. You don't have to say yes to everything, but you don't have to treat everyone as a threat either.

## Your Quick Checklist

- Know the fair price before you need it.
- Use official counters and apps for tickets and taxis.
- Agree every price before the service starts.
- Say "no shops" before getting into any vehicle.
- Never let strangers handle your card or bag.
- If you feel rushed, slow down.
